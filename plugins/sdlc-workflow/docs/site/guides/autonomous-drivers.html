Autonomous drivers

This page shows how to run a whole workflow with one command. Use /wf auto to answer every gate yourself, or /wf yolo for an autonomous run that reports its decisions back for audit.

Commands on this site are written as /wf …. Under Codex, type $wf … instead. The key and the arguments are the same.

After intake, shape, and (when the work needs design) the human-only design stage, the remaining stages are slice, plan, implement, verify, and review. Those stages mostly run in order, and each stage surfaces its own issues. The two drivers automate that sequencing. Neither driver is a stage: a driver writes no artifact of its own. Every file in .ai/workflows/<slug>/ is written by the stage that the driver delegated to, exactly as if you typed the stage command yourself. The only difference between the two drivers is who answers the gates.

/wf auto — the human-gated driver

Start the driver with a slug:

/wf auto <slug>

auto reads the workflow's control file, determines which stage runs next, and executes that stage in-process. It follows the stage's own reference verbatim. When the stage finishes cleanly, auto reads the artifact that the stage just wrote and moves on to the next stage. When a stage's own gate fires, auto pauses the chain and hands control back to you. A gate fires when verify escalates a failing check, when review leaves an open blocker, or when a stage records a pending product-owner question. The hand-back summarizes what ran, why the run stopped, and the command that resumes the run.

auto does not answer those gates for you. Each delegated stage keeps its own user interaction: verify still runs its fix/skip/escalate loop with you, and review still asks you to fix, defer, or dismiss its findings. auto removes the friction of typing each stage command by hand. It never removes, suppresses, or pre-answers a gate. Because the human is present, a delegated stage asks any intent-bearing decision at its gate and does not resolve it silently. Only implementation-detail decisions are auto-resolved, and those decisions are recorded.

The driver has two modes, chosen by whether you name a slice:

/wf auto <slug>
Slug mode. The driver moves every slice in the roster forward, then runs the final review, then stops. With per-slice review scope, each slice runs plan → implement → verify → review. With slug-wide scope, each slice runs plan → implement → verify, and one review of the whole branch diff runs at the end.
/wf auto <slug> <slice>
Slice mode. The driver moves just that one slice to its endpoint, then routes you to the next slice in the roster.

Resume is free. Every stage updates the control file and writes its own artifact, so the artifact trail is the run log. If a run is interrupted, run /wf auto <slug> again. The driver continues from the point where the artifacts show that the run stopped. There is no separate state file to lose.

/wf yolo — the autonomous driver

/wf yolo <slug> does what auto does, with one difference. Where auto pauses at a gate, yolo resolves the gate itself by a written decision policy, records the decision into the artifact, and drives on. When you type the command, you explicitly opt in to an unattended run. The same two modes apply: slug mode drives everything to the review, and slice mode drives one slice.

The policy has two tiers, by design. Most gates have an auto-resolve rule:

  • At a plan fork, the driver picks the option that best satisfies the slice's acceptance criteria at the least cost, and it records the assumption.
  • At a failing verify, the driver applies the minimal fix and allows up to two rounds.
  • At review triage, the driver fixes every blocker, high, and medium finding. It defers a low-severity finding only when the fix would reach outside the slice, and it always records the reason.

Some gates are hard stops, even for an autonomous driver:

  • a fork that would change user-observable scope or a public contract
  • a substantive failure that is still unresolved after the fix rounds
  • a dont-ship review verdict
  • an unfixable security or data-loss blocker
  • a standing-steering veto in the workflow's steer.md

These gates end the run with the artifact trail and a stated reason, because they are product decisions, not best-interest calls.

Three mechanisms keep an unattended run honest:

  • Decision classification. Every autonomous decision is classified as implementation-detail or intent-bearing. An intent-bearing decision is never lawfully autonomous; by policy, an intent-bearing decision must be a stop. At the end of the run, the decisionDigest in the hand-back groups every decision by class. The digest explicitly names any intent-bearing decision that resolved itself, so a mis-classified auto-resolve cannot hide inside the artifact trail. Audit those decisions first.
  • The driver journal. Every subagent that the driver dispatches appends a heartbeat line to .ai/workflows/<slug>/.driver-journal.jsonl when the subagent starts and when it finishes. A journal that stops growing is the signal that the driver stopped. /wf status <slug> reads the journal and reports the driver as running, presumed dead, or completed. The report is judged by the recency of the heartbeat lines, not by the existence of the file.
  • Deferral, never fabrication. Sometimes an acceptance criterion needs runtime proof that the environment genuinely cannot produce, and the incapability was probed, not assumed. In that case, yolo defers that one criterion through verify's own sanctioned escape hatch. The deferral is recorded in the control file, blocks ship downstream, and carries the probe's output as a receipt. A criterion that the driver actually exercised and found wrong is a failure, never a deferral.

The watch and the running commentary

When yolo starts a run, it also starts a watch on that run. The watch polls the driver journal, the branch's commits, a set of protected files, and the account usage readings. Each event comes back to the session that started the run. That session then gives you a running commentary: a short note when a stage starts, a full note when a stage ends (what it built, what it decided, how many errors it recovered from, and what comes next), and one or two sentences per commit. Each note is also appended to .ai/workflows/<slug>/commentary.md, so the story survives a compaction and you can read it later.

Some events change what you do next, so they also send a push notification: a decision that needs you, a change to a protected file, a driver that went silent, a stop, and the end of the run. The protected files are PRODUCT.md, DESIGN.md, every file that held your uncommitted edits when the run started, the paths that a do-not-touch line in steer.md names, and the yolo.protectedFiles list in .ai/sdlc-config.json. Say "quiet" to keep only the stop, silence, and end notes.

You can stop the run at a stage boundary. Say "stop after the current verify" or "stop after this stage". The session writes the request to .ai/workflows/<slug>/.control.json. The agent that starts the next stage reads that file, does no work, and the run ends cleanly. The hand-back gives the resume command. A usage pause uses the same file: when a usage window is nearly full, the run pauses at the next stage boundary and resumes after the window resets.

yolo refuses terminal-analysis workflows: investigate, discover, ideate, audit, and brainstorm. Those workflows deliberately end in a human product decision: pick an option, act on a verdict, choose an idea, accept a finding, or continue a thought. That decision is exactly the intake-and-shape alignment that an autonomous driver must not perform. yolo hands back the recorded next step instead. yolo also refuses task workflows, for a different reason: a task is gated on authorization to act — its shared-environment, external-party, and irreversible classes stop for a human precisely because no written policy should resolve them unattended. Both refusals hold for auto too: auto pauses and routes you to /wf task <slug> or /wf intake audit <slug>. yolo also never runs intake, shape, or design itself: it drives an already-shaped slug from plan onward.

Neither driver runs the design stage. When the work needs design and a person has not confirmed it, auto pauses and routes you to /wf design <slug>, and yolo refuses to start. When a driven stage cannot build the confirmed design as drawn, the run stops and routes you to /wf design <slug> amend. See Design.

/wf yolo runs under Claude Code only. It is built on a background-workflow tool that Codex does not have. Under Codex the key is unknown. /wf auto runs under both hosts. See Hosts.

When to use which

Use auto when you want to stay in the loop: you are at the keyboard, the work is unfamiliar or risky, and you want to see each verify result and review finding as it lands. Use yolo when the slug is well-shaped and the decisions ahead are mostly implementation detail. In that case, you inspect a recorded pile of decisions afterward instead of answering each one live. A useful middle path exists: before the run, put your standing constraints and preferences into the workflow's steer.md. Both drivers read that file. In an unattended run, steer.md is the only user voice present, so a steering veto outranks every auto-resolve default.

What neither driver will ever do

Both drivers always stop before handoff. Neither driver opens a pull request. Neither driver runs /wf handoff, /wf ship, or /wf retro; those are deliberate, separately invoked steps. Neither driver fixes CI; that stays handoff's diagnose-then-ask job. Neither driver removes a gate: every stage's quality gate still runs under both drivers. auto waits for your answer. yolo supplies the answer that the policy allows and records that answer. The gates themselves are untouched, which is why a driver run is exactly as auditable as a hand-driven run.

/wf campaign — many slugs from one brainstorm

A brainstorm that ends in many pieces of work leaves one work packet per piece in its work/ folder. /wf campaign <brainstorm-slug> drives those packets in dependency waves, so you do not keep the order in your head. Setup asks you four questions: whether you want the forecast, the target version, whether each wave releases, and which outputs to build. You then prepare each slug with the campaign: intake, shape, slice, and the design stage when the packet needs one. The campaign never answers an intake or shape question for you.

Each wave runs yolo on its slugs one at a time, so every yolo rule above still holds. The campaign then merges the slugs into one wave branch, verifies the merge, and writes an as-built note per slug that a second agent tries to refute. When a merge breaks the wave verify, the campaign reverts the merges newest first until the verify passes, and the other slugs stay merged. The wave then goes through /wf handoff and /wf ship as one PR. This is the one place where the slugs reach a PR without you typing the command, and the campaign asks you at every handoff or ship issue: red CI, a required approval, a version question, or a recovery step that deletes a tag. Before the next wave, a drift check compares what the earlier slugs built with what the next slugs expect, and a contract difference stops the slugs that depend on it until you decide.

Three things make a campaign faster and safer when the project allows them. An isolation contract (campaign.isolation in .ai/sdlc-config.json) gives each slug of a wave its own worktree, its own ports, and its own build folders, so up to three slugs drive at once; a test suite that cannot share the machine runs under a lock, one worktree at a time. With gh-stack v0.1.0 or later, the wave PRs form a stack, so the next wave starts on the wave below while that wave waits for review, with at most two unshipped waves. And the usage guard in the sdlc mod reads your 5-hour and 7-day windows: at 75% the campaign starts no new slug while another runs, at 90% it pauses until the reset, and it never uses the last 15% of your week. When the window resets, the guard resumes the campaign by itself.

The state lives in work/campaign/ledger.json inside the brainstorm, so the same command resumes at any point. Keep adding to the brainstorm in another session with /wf brainstorm <brainstorm-slug> add <text>; the campaign picks up the new packets at the next wave boundary. Setup and prepare run on every host. The waves run on Claude Code only.
